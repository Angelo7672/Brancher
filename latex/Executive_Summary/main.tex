\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Human Pose Estimation (HPE) is a fundamental task in computer vision that aims to localize human body joints—such as head, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles—within image or video frames. These joints are 
subsequently connected to form a skeletal representation of the human body. Depending on the target space, HPE can be formulated as a 2D problem, where joint positions are estimated in the image plane, or as a 3D problem, where 
joints are reconstructed in three-dimensional space. Accurate human pose estimation plays a crucial role in enabling machines to understand human posture, movement, and behavior, thus supporting a wide range of applications 
including healthcare, surveillance, human-computer interaction, and sports performance analysis.\\
In recent years, HPE has experienced significant advancements driven by deep learning techniques, achieving remarkable accuracy in controlled environments. Modern neural architectures are capable of producing highly reliable pose 
estimates when operating under favorable conditions, such as limited occlusions, well-defined camera viewpoints, and sufficient training data. Despite these achievements, several challenges remain unresolved, particularly in 
unconstrained scenarios. Occlusions, limited viewpoint diversity, depth ambiguity in monocular settings, and the scarcity of annotated 3D data still represent major obstacles for robust and generalizable HPE systems.\\
Sports analysis constitutes one of the most demanding application domains for pose estimation. In indoor sports, effective solutions have been developed by leveraging fixed multi-camera systems, which enable accurate 3D reconstruction 
through geometric triangulation. However, this approach becomes significantly more challenging in outdoor sports environments, where installing and calibrating a dense network of static cameras is often impractical or infeasible. 
As a result, traditional motion capture systems struggle to scale beyond controlled laboratory conditions and fail to capture natural athletic movements in real-world settings.\\
In parallel with the evolution of pose estimation methods, the past decade has witnessed substantial technological progress in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), commonly known as drones. Initially designed for basic aerial imaging 
tasks, early drones offered limited autonomy and required extensive manual control. Advances in onboard sensors, flight control algorithms, gimbal stabilization, and edge computing have progressively transformed drones into 
intelligent, semi-autonomous aerial platforms. Modern consumer-grade drones are now capable of autonomous navigation, subject tracking, real-time video acquisition, and on-device processing, making them suitable for complex 
vision-based applications.\\
The combination of drone technology and computer vision algorithms introduces new opportunities for human pose estimation in outdoor sports contexts. Aerial viewpoints allow the capture of motion data in environments where fixed 
cameras are impractical, enabling large-scale and flexible data acquisition. However, drone-based HPE introduces additional challenges, including dynamic camera motion, varying viewpoints, scale changes, and the inherent ambiguity 
of monocular 3D reconstruction.

\section{Related Work}
This section reviews only deep learning-based methods, focusing on single-pose estimation.

\subsection{2D Human Pose Estimation}
The earliest deep learning approach for 2D human pose estimation is DeepPose, which formulates pose estimation as a regression problem.
In contrast, most modern methods adopt a detection-based formulation, where joint locations are inferred from heatmap representations. High-Resolution Networks (HRNet) maintain high-resolution feature representations throughout the 
network by processing multiple resolutions in parallel. ViTPose leverages Transformer-based architectures to model long-range spatial dependencies
single images. While these models operate on single images, several methods extend 2D HPE to video sequences by explicitly exploiting temporal information. Dynamic Kernel Distillation (DKD) propagates pose-aware 
features from previous frames to the current frame to enforce temporal consistency. Key Frame Proposal Network (K-FPN) selects the most informative frames in a video and learns a dynamic model to reconstruct poses in the remaining 
frames.\\
In the sports field, Ski Pose Estimation adds ski's keypoints to sky videos, and AI Coach offers personalized feedback based on movement quality.

\subsection{3D Human Pose Estimation}
SelfPose is an image-based, self-supervised approach for 3D egocentric pose estimation. The proposed architecture consists of two main components: a 2D pose detector that predicts joint heatmaps from an input RGB image, and a 
multi-branch autoencoder that learns a latent pose representation while explicitly modeling per-joint prediction uncertainty. By relying on self-supervision, SelfPose reduces the need for large-scale annotated 3D datasets and 
operates on individual images.
In contrast, several recent methods formulate 3D HPE as a \textit{sequence-to-sequence} problem, leveraging temporal context from video data. MixSTE is among the first approaches to employ an encoder-decoder framework based on 
Transformer blocks to map 2D pose sequences to 3D pose sequences.
MotionBERT further advances this paradigm by learning a generic representation of human motion from large-scale and heterogeneous 2D skeleton datasets. The model is first pretrained as a motion encoder using unlabeled 2D pose sequences 
and subsequently fine-tuned for downstream 3D pose estimation tasks.
MotionAGFormer adopts a hybrid Transformer-Graph architecture, where Graph Convolutional Networks capture local spatial and temporal relationships between joints, while Transformer layers model global motion dependencies across the 
sequence.\\
Recent works have specifically investigated 3D human pose estimation from UAV platforms, aiming to enable pose analysis in outdoor environments using aerial imagery.
AirPose proposes a decentralized and distributed framework for 3D human pose and shape estimation from uncalibrated, moving cameras. The system jointly estimates camera calibration relative to the human subject and reconstructs 3D pose 
by combining complementary information from multiple aerial viewpoints, thereby improving robustness and accuracy.
Aerial View, in contrast, addresses 3D human pose estimation from monocular images captured by a single drone. The method relies on a double Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) architecture: one VQ-VAE is trained to learn 
a discrete codebook of 3D human poses, while a second VQ-VAE encodes visual features from aerial images. The learned visual representations are then mapped to the 3D pose codebook, enabling pose reconstruction from single-view aerial 
images.\\
However, these approaches do not explicitly exploit temporal information or are not designed for single-pose sports analysis from drone videos.


\section{Problem Formulation}
The goal of this thesis is to estimate the 3D motion of an athlete from a monocular drone footage. Let $x$ be a video sequence of an athlete captured by a drone-mounted monocular camera, the problem is to learn a function $f_\theta$, 
that maps the input video to a sequence of 3D human poses:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\theta}: \mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times W \times C} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{T \times J \times (x,y,z)}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the temporal length of the video, $H$ and $W$ are the spatial dimensions of each frame, $C$ is the number of channels, $J$ denotes the number of body joints, and \((x,y,z)\) are the 3D coordinates of a joint.
The learning objective is to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth joint positions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MPJPE} = \frac{1}{T} \frac{1}{J} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \| \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} - \mathbf{y}_{t,j} \|_2
\end{equation}


\section{Proposed Method}
In my project, I proposed a deep learning-based framework for 3D human pose estimation from drone videos named \textbf{Brancher}. The goal of Brancher is to accurately reconstruct the 3D motion of an athlete from monocular aerial 
footage, addressing the challenges posed by dynamic camera motion, varying viewpoints, and scale changes inherent in drone-based video capture.

\subsection{Preprocess}
The preprocess operation is a fundamental step in training with video data, although the feedforward of the model will be very heavy and long if we input the raw video directly into the model.
It consists of extracting human's bounding boxes from each frame of the video sequence and from these boxes, extracting the 2D keypoints of the human body.
For the first task, I have used YOLOv12, a state-of-the-art object detection model that provides accurate and real-time detection of humans in images and videos.
For the second task, I have used ViTPose, a high-resolution network for 2D human pose estimation.
The 2d keypoints are then normalized in the range [-1, 1] based on the width and height of the frame.

\subsection{Baseline}
As a baseline model for 3D human pose estimation from drone videos, I have implemented a simple architecture based on temporal transformer. The 2d keypoints normalized are embedded into a higher dimensional space using a linear layer, 
and encoded with positional encoding to retain temporal information in a tensor $[B, T, D]$. The encoded features are then passed through a series of transformer encoder layers to capture temporal dependencies across frames.
Finally, a linear layer maps the output features to 3D joint coordinates for each frame using a tanh activation function to ensure the output is in the range [-1, 1].

\subsection{Spatial and Temporal Attention Module}
To better capture both spatial and temporal dependencies in the input sequence of 2D keypoints, inspired by MotionAGFormer, I used a transformer-graph hybrid architecture tailored for 3D HPE. Transformers to capture global information
while simultaneously employing Graph Convolutional Networks to integrate local spatial and temporal relationships.

\subsection{Multibranch Network}
To effectively captures motion dynamics from a drone footage, where the camera is constantly moving and the scale of the athlete varies significantly, I designed a multibranch architecture based on SelfPose. Each branch is specialized 
in predicting different tasks in a self supervised manner.

\paragraph{3D Pose Branch}
This branch is responsible for regressing the 3D joint coordinates from the latent space produced by the spatial and temporal attention module. It uses an MLP architecture followed by a tanh activation function to map the encoded 
features to 3D joint positions. It uses the MPJPE loss function during training, and it is the only supervised loss. In others it uses three self-supervised losses.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{bone} = \frac{1}{B} [\text{Var}_{t}(l_{bone})]
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{vel} &= \frac{1}{(T-2)J} \\
    &\quad \sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \| (\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t+1,j}} - \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}}) + \\
    &\quad - (\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} - \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t-1,j}}) \|_2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{acc} &= \frac{1}{(T-3)J} \\
    &\quad \sum_{t=2}^{T-2} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \| (\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t+1,j}} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} + \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t-1,j}}) + \\
    &\quad - (\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t-1,j}} + \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t-2,j}}) \|_2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Rotation Branch}
This branch estimates the rotation of every joints with respect to the T-Pose. It uses an MLP architecture using a 6D rotation representation and then converts it to a rotation matrix using the 
Gram-Schmidt process. The predicted rotation matrix is compared with the one computed from the predicted 3D joints using the geodesic loss.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{geo} = \frac{1}{B} \text{arccos} \left( \frac{\text{tr}(R_{\text{pred}} R_{\text{target}}^T) - 1}{2} \right)
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Uncertainty Branch}
This branch estimates the uncertainty associated with each predicted joint by predicting a heatmap representation for each joint.


The different loss are weighted with learnable parameters during the training phase using the homoscedastic uncertainty method.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{total} = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \mathcal{L}_i + \log \sigma_i
\end{equation}





\section{Guidelines}
\label{sec:guidelines}

The Executive Summary is a critical overview of your thesis
with a focus on the main achievements that have emerged from your research.

The Executive Summary should be organized in sections/paragraphs
in order to better highlight the major points of your work.
The length should range from four to six pages depending on the length of the thesis manuscript.
Keep the Executive Summary concise enough to be effective but long enough to allow it to be complete.
It should be written after completing the thesis manuscript as a stand-alone independent document
of sufficient clarity and detail to ensure that the reader can figure out the overall objectives,
the methodology employed and the results/impact of your research.

In writing the Executive Summary, keep in mind that it is not an abstract, it is not a preface,
and it is not a random collection of highlights.
With a few exceptions, do not simply cut and paste whole sections or paragraphs of the thesis manuscript
into a disorganized and cluttered Executive Summary.
You should reorganize information to be informative as well as concise.

The Executive Summary could contain a few important equations related to your work.
It could also include the most relevant figures and tables taken or elaborated from the thesis manuscript.

You should also include in the Executive Summary the very essential bibliography of your study.
The number of selected references should range from three to five depending on the type of work.

The Executive Summary should contain a final section reporting the main conclusions drawn from your research.
