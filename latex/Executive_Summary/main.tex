\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Human Pose Estimation (HPE) is a cornerstone of modern computer vision, built upon keypoint detection, where human body joints, such as head, shoulders, elbows and wrists, are modeled as structured semantic landmarks and localized in 
images or video sequences to reconstruct the geometry of the human body.
Depending on the target space, HPE can be formulated as a 2D problem, where joint positions are estimated in the image plane, or as a 3D problem, where joints are reconstructed in three-dimensional space. 
Accurate human pose estimation plays a crucial role in enabling machines to understand human posture, movement, and behavior, thus supporting a wide range of applications including healthcare, surveillance, human-computer interaction, 
and sports performance analysis.\\
In recent years, HPE has experienced significant advancements driven by deep learning techniques, achieving remarkable accuracy in controlled environments. Modern neural architectures are capable of producing highly reliable pose 
estimates when operating under favorable conditions, such as limited occlusions, well-defined camera viewpoints, and sufficient training data. Despite these achievements, several challenges remain unresolved, particularly in 
unconstrained scenarios. Occlusions, limited viewpoint diversity, depth ambiguity in monocular settings, and the scarcity of annotated 3D data still represent major obstacles for robust and generalizable HPE systems.\\
Sports analysis constitutes one of the most demanding application domains for pose estimation. In indoor sports, effective solutions have been developed by leveraging fixed multi-camera systems, which enable accurate 3D reconstruction 
through geometric triangulation. However, this approach becomes significantly more challenging in outdoor sports environments, where installing and calibrating a dense network of static cameras is often impractical or infeasible. 
As a result, traditional motion capture systems fail to capture natural athletic movements in real-world settings.\\
In parallel with advances in pose estimation, the past decade has seen rapid progress in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). Originally limited to basic aerial imaging with manual control, modern drones have evolved into intelligent, 
semi-autonomous platforms thanks to improvements in onboard sensing, flight control, gimbal stabilization, and edge computing. Today, consumer-grade drones support autonomous navigation, subject tracking, and real-time video acquisition, 
making them well suited for advanced vision-based applications.\\
The combination of drone technology and computer vision algorithms introduces new opportunities for human pose estimation in outdoor sports contexts. Aerial viewpoints allow the capture of motion data in environments where fixed 
cameras are impractical, enabling large-scale and flexible data acquisition. However, drone-based HPE introduces additional challenges, including dynamic camera motion, varying viewpoints, scale changes, and the inherent ambiguity 
of monocular 3D reconstruction.

This thesis introduces Brancher, a self-supervised neural network for single-person, single-view 3D HPE from drone videos. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brancher Architecture:} I propose a novel self-supervised framework initially trained on high-speed sports videos to learn motion-aware pose representations.
    \item \textbf{Self-Supervised Drone Adaptation:} I introduce a self-supervised fine-tuning strategy tailored to drone videos, addressing domain shift, viewpoint variability, and scale changes typical of aerial recordings
    without requiring 3D ground-truth annotations.
    \item \textbf{Custom Drone Dataset:} I collect a novel drone-based sports video dataset, specifically designed for 3D HPE in outdoor environments.
\end{itemize}
Together, these contributions aim to bridge the gap between state-of-the-art 3D pose estimation methods and real-world drone-based sports analysis.


\section{Related Work}
This section reviews deep learning-based methods for 3D Human Pose Estimation, with a particular focus on single-person scenarios.

Several recent approaches formulate 3D HPE as a \textit{sequence-to-sequence} problem, leveraging temporal context from video data to improve robustness and accuracy. Within this paradigm, MotionAGFormer \cite{mehraban2024motionagformer} 
adopts a hybrid Transformer-Graph architecture, where Graph Convolutional Networks capture local spatial and temporal relationships between joints, while Transformer layers model long-range 
dependencies and global motion patterns across the sequence.\\
More recently, research attention has shifted toward 3D HPE from aerial platforms, aiming to enable pose analysis in outdoor environments using UAV-acquired imagery. AirPose \cite{saini2022airpose} proposes a decentralized and distributed 
framework for 3D HPE and shape estimation from multiple uncalibrated and moving cameras. The method jointly estimates camera calibration relative to the human subject and reconstructs the 3D pose by aggregating complementary 
information from multiple aerial viewpoints, resulting in improved robustness and accuracy.
In contrast, Aerial View \cite{hwang2024aerial} addresses the more challenging setting of monocular 3D HPE from images captured by a single drone. The approach employs a double Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) architecture, 
where one model learns a discrete codebook of plausible 3D human poses and the other encodes visual features extracted from aerial images. The learned visual representations are then mapped to the 3D pose codebook, enabling pose 
reconstruction from single-view aerial imagery.

However, existing drone-based approaches either do not explicitly exploit temporal information or are not specifically designed for single-person sports analysis from drone videos, leaving room for further investigation in this domain.


\section{Problem Formulation}
The goal of this thesis is to estimate the 3D motion of an athlete from a monocular drone footage. Let $x$ be a video sequence of an athlete captured by a drone-mounted monocular camera, the problem is to learn a function $f_\theta$, 
that maps the input video to a sequence of 3D human poses:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\theta}: \mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times W \times C} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{T \times J \times P}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the temporal length of the video, $H$ and $W$ are the spatial dimensions of each frame, $C$ is the number of channels, $J$ denotes the number of body joints, and $P$ is the number of coordinates for each joint (e.g., 3 for 
$(x,y,z)$).
The learning objective is to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth joint positions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MPJPE} = \frac{1}{T} \frac{1}{J} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \| \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} - \mathbf{y}_{t,j} \|_2
\end{equation}
\label{eq:mpjpe}
where $\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}}$ and $\mathbf{y}_{t,j}$ are the predicted and ground-truth 3D coordinates of joint $j$ at frame $t$, respectively.

\section{Proposed Method}
In my thesis, I proposed a deep learning-based framework for 3D human pose estimation from drone videos named \textbf{Brancher}. The goal of Brancher is to accurately reconstruct the 3D motion of an athlete from monocular aerial 
footage, addressing the challenges posed by dynamic camera motion, varying viewpoints, and scale changes inherent in drone-based video capture.\\
I will first introduce the preprocess step that extracts 2D keypoints from the raw video input, then I will describe the baseline model used as reference for the ablation study, and finally I will present the main components of 
Brancher.

\subsection{Preprocess}
The preprocess operation is a fundamental step in training with video data, although the feedforward of the model will be very heavy and long if the input is the raw video directly.
It consists of extracting human's bounding boxes from each frame of the video sequence and from these boxes, extracting the 2D keypoints of the human body.
For the first task, I have used YOLOv12, an object detection model that provides accurate and real-time detection of humans in images and videos.
For 2D pose estimation, I employ ViTPose \cite{xu2022vitpose}, a high-resolution transformer-based architecture for human keypoint detection.
Given an input frame, this network outputs $J$ heatmaps, where $J$ corresponds to the number of predefined body joints. Each heatmap represents the spatial probability distribution of a specific joint within the image.
The 2D joint coordinates $(x,y)$ are extracted from the predicted heatmaps using an \textit{argmax} operation, which identifies the location of the maximum response. The corresponding maximum activation value is interpreted as a 
confidence score in the range $[0,1]$, reflecting the reliability of the joint prediction.

The 2D keypoints are then normalized in the range $x \in [-1, 1]$ and $y \in [-\frac{H}{W}, \frac{H}{W}]$ based on the width $W$ and height $H$ of the frame.\\
The normalized joint coordinates, the associated confidence scores, and the full set of predicted heatmaps are stored on disk and subsequently used as input representations during the training phase of the proposed 3D pose 
estimation model.

\subsection{Baseline}
As a baseline for monocular 3D HPE from drone videos, I implement a lightweight architecture based solely on a \textbf{temporal Transformer encoder}, without any 
explicit spatial attention mechanism. The input consists of normalized 2D keypoints for each frame in a temporal window of length $T$. 
The 2D joint coordinates are first linearly projected into a latent embedding space of dimension $D$ through a fully connected layer. 
The resulting sequence of embeddings is then enriched with a standard sinusoidal positional encoding (sine-cosine formulation) to preserve temporal order, producing a 
tensor of shape $[T,D]$. 
The encoded sequence is processed by a stack of Transformer encoder layers operating exclusively along the temporal dimension, enabling the model to capture motion 
dynamics and long-range temporal dependencies across frames.
Finally, the output features are mapped to 3D joint coordinates for each frame through a regression head, referred to as \textit{PoseHead}, implemented as a multilayer 
perceptron (MLP) with a \textit{tanh} activation function. The PoseHead performs frame-wise 3D regression from the latent temporal representation, producing the 
estimated 3D joint positions. The baseline framework is shown in figure \ref{fig:baseline}.\\
Although this architecture effectively models temporal dependencies, it presents several limitations. First, the absence of explicit spatial modeling prevents the 
network from directly capturing structural relationships between joints. Second, the model relies entirely on supervised regression from 2D to 3D poses, without 
leveraging self-supervised constraints or geometric priors. Finally, its design does not explicitly address the domain shift introduced by aerial viewpoints in 
drone-acquired footage.
These limitations motivate the development of the proposed Brancher architecture.

\subsection{Brancher}
\useSvgWithCaption[fig:brancher]{Images/brancher.svg}{1.0}{1.0}{Brancher model architecture}
Brancher is a self-supervised architecture designed for single-person, single-view 3D human pose estimation from drone videos. Starting from normalized 2D keypoints, 
the model first processes the input through a joint spatial-temporal attention module, enabling the learning of both structural relationships between joints and motion 
dynamics across frames.
The resulting representation is then fed into a multi-branch refinement stage, where specialized branches are dedicated to addressing the key challenges of drone-based 
footage, including viewpoint variability, scale changes, and motion-induced artifacts. By jointly modeling spatial structure, temporal coherence, and drone-specific 
characteristics, Brancher aims to produce robust 3D pose estimates in challenging aerial scenarios.

\subsubsection{Spatial and Temporal Attention Module}
A key limitation of purely temporal transformer-based models is that they treat each joint independently across time, without explicitly modeling the structural 
relationships of the human body. 
However, in drone-based scenarios, strong perspective changes, scale variations, and partial occlusions make it essential to jointly reason about 
\textit{which joints belong together} and \textit{how they move over time}.
To address this issue, I adopt a spatial-temporal attention module inspired by MotionAGFormer \cite{mehraban2024motionagformer}, which combines the strengths of 
transformers and graph-based modeling.
The transformer layers are responsible for capturing long-range temporal dependencies across the video sequence, enabling the model to reason about motion patterns 
over multiple frames.
In parallel, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are used to explicitly encode the spatial structure of the human skeleton, enforcing anatomical constraints and local 
joint correlations.
This hybrid design allows the network to attend both to \textit{global temporal dynamics} and \textit{local spatial relationships} in a unified framework.
As a result, the model becomes more robust to viewpoint changes and noisy 2D keypoint detections, which are common in monocular drone footage.


\subsubsection{Multibranch Network}
To robustly model human motion from monocular drone footage, where camera motion, perspective distortion, and scale variation strongly affect the observed 2D keypoints, I propose a multibranch architecture inspired by SelfPose \cite{tome2020selfpose}.
Instead of relying on a single regression objective, the model decomposes the problem into multiple correlated tasks, each enforcing complementary geometric and kinematic constraints.

All branches share the same spatial-temporal latent representation and are trained jointly.
This design enables cross-task regularization, improves training stability, and significantly reduces the reliance on fully supervised 3D annotations, which are expensive and scarce in real-world aerial scenarios.

\paragraph{3D Pose Branch}
The 3D Pose Branch is responsible for regressing the 3D joint coordinates from the latent representation produced by the spatial-temporal attention module.
It consists of a lightweight MLP followed by a \textit{tanh} activation function, which maps the encoded features to root-relative 3D joint positions.

During training, this branch is supervised by the Mean Per Joint Position Error (\hyperref[eq:mpjpe]{MPJPE}), which directly penalizes discrepancies with ground-truth 3D poses and provides the strongest supervision signal.
In addition, three self-supervised losses are employed to enforce physical plausibility and temporal consistency of the predicted motion.

The bone length loss, where $B$ is the number of bones in the skeleton, enforces temporal consistency of skeletal proportions by penalizing variations in bone lengths across frames:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{bone} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \text{Var}_{t} \bigl( \ell_{b}^{(t)} \bigr)
\end{equation}

The velocity loss encourages smooth motion by penalizing abrupt changes in joint velocities:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{vel} &= \frac{1}{(T-2)J} 
    \sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \\
    &\quad \left\| (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1,j} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j}) 
    - (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j}) \right\|_2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Similarly, the acceleration loss penalizes strong variations in joint accelerations, further enforcing temporal smoothness:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{acc} &= \frac{1}{(T-3)J}
    \sum_{t=3}^{T-1} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \\
    &\quad \left\| \begin{aligned}
        &(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1,j} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j}) + \\
        &\quad - (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-2,j})
    \end{aligned} \right\|_2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Rotation Branch}
While the 3D Pose Branch captures the global joint configuration, it does not explicitly enforce rotational consistency of the skeletal segments.
To address this limitation, the Rotation Branch predicts the relative orientation of each bone with respect to a canonical T-pose.

This branch is implemented as an MLP that outputs a 6D rotation representation for each bone, which is subsequently mapped to a valid rotation matrix $ \mathbf{\hat{R}} \in SO(3) $ using a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process.
The 6D representation is chosen for its continuity properties, ensuring stable learning and avoiding the discontinuities of Euler angles and quaternions.

Let $ P \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times J \times 3} $ denote the 3D joint predictions.
The function $r(P)$ produces a set of rotation matrices $ \mathbb{R} \in SO(3)^{T \times O} $ by aligning each T-pose bone vector with its corresponding predicted bone vector, where $O = 13$ is the number of bones chosen for this task.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{geo} = \arccos \left( \frac{\text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}} \mathbf{R}^\top) - 1}{2} \right)
\end{equation}
This loss enforces consistency between joint positions and bone orientations, providing an additional geometric constraint that improves the overall robustness of the motion reconstruction.



\paragraph{Uncertainty Branch}
This branch estimates the uncertainty associated with each predicted joint by predicting a heatmap representation for each joint.

\subsection{Loss Weighting}
The different loss terms are combined using learnable weights during training, following the homoscedastic uncertainty formulation for multi-task learning.
Instead of manually tuning fixed scalar weights for each objective, the network learns a task-dependent uncertainty parameter $\sigma_i$ that automatically balances the contribution of each loss.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{total} = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \mathcal{L}_i + \log \sigma_i
\end{equation}
