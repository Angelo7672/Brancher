\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Human Pose Estimation (HPE) is a cornerstone of modern computer vision, built upon keypoint detection, where human body joints are modeled as semantic keypoints and localized in 
images or video sequences to reconstruct the geometry of the human body.
Depending on the target space, HPE can be formulated as a 2D problem, where joint positions are estimated in the image plane, or as a 3D problem, where joints are reconstructed in 3D space. 
Accurate HPE plays a crucial role in enabling machines to understand human movement, supporting a wide range of applications such as healthcare, surveillance and sports performance analysis.\\
Recently, HPE has improved significantly driven by deep learning techniques, achieving great accuracy in controlled environments. Nevertheless, several challenges remain unresolved, particularly in 
unconstrained scenarios. Occlusions, limited viewpoint diversity, depth ambiguity in monocular settings, and the scarcity of annotated 3D data represent major obstacles for robust and generalizable HPE systems.\\
Sports analysis constitutes one of the most demanding application domains for HPE. In indoor sports, effective solutions have been developed employing fixed multi-camera systems, which enable accurate 3D reconstruction 
through geometric triangulation. However, this approach becomes challenging in outdoor sports environments, where installing and calibrating static cameras is often impractical. 
As a result, traditional motion capture systems fail to capture athletic movements in real-world settings.\\
In parallel with advances in pose estimation, the past decade has seen rapid progress in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). Today, consumer-grade drones support autonomous navigation, subject tracking, and real-time video acquisition, 
making them well suited for advanced vision-based applications.\\
The combination of drone technology and computer vision algorithms introduces new opportunities for HPE in outdoor sports contexts. UAVs allow capturing of motion data in environments where fixed 
cameras are impractical, enabling large-scale and flexible data acquisition. However, drone-based HPE introduces additional challenges such as dynamic camera motion, varying viewpoints, scale changes, and the ambiguity 
of monocular 3D reconstruction.

This thesis introduces Brancher, a self-supervised neural network for single-person, single-view 3D HPE from drone videos. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a novel self-supervised framework initially trained on high-speed sports videos to learn motion-aware pose representations.
    \item We introduce a self-supervised fine-tuning strategy tailored to drone videos, addressing domain shift, viewpoint variability, and scale changes without requiring 3D ground-truth annotations.
    \item We collect a novel drone-based video dataset, specifically designed for 3D HPE in outdoor environments.
\end{itemize}
These contributions aim to bridge the gap between state-of-the-art 3D pose estimation methods and real-world drone-based sports analysis.


\section{Related Work}
Several recent approaches formulate 3D HPE as a \textit{sequence-to-sequence} problem, leveraging temporal context from video data to improve robustness and accuracy. Within this paradigm, MotionAGFormer \cite{mehraban2024motionagformer} 
adopts a hybrid Transformer-Graph architecture, where Graph Convolutional Networks capture local spatial and temporal relationships between joints, while Transformer layers model long-range 
dependencies and global motion patterns across the sequence.\\
More recently, research attention has shifted toward 3D HPE from aerial platforms, aiming to enable pose analysis in outdoor environments using UAV-acquired imagery. AirPose \cite{saini2022airpose} proposes a decentralized and distributed 
framework for 3D HPE and shape estimation from multiple uncalibrated and moving cameras. The method jointly estimates camera calibration relative to the human subject and reconstructs the 3D pose by aggregating complementary 
information from multiple aerial viewpoints, resulting in improved robustness and accuracy.

Existing drone-based approaches either do not explicitly exploit temporal information or are not specifically designed for single-person sports analysis from drone videos, leaving room for further investigation in this domain.


\section{Problem Formulation}
Our goal is to estimate the 3D motion of an athlete from a monocular drone footage. Let $x$ be an RGB video sequence of an athlete captured by a drone-mounted monocular camera, and $y$ a sequence of 3D coordinates root-relative. We aim to 
learn a function $f_\theta$, 
that maps the input video to a sequence of 3D human poses:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\theta}: x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times W \times C} \rightarrow y \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times J \times 3}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the temporal length of the video, $H$ and $W$ are the spatial dimensions of each frame, $C$ is the number of channels, $J$ denotes the number of joints each having 3 coordinates $(x,y,z)$.
The learning objective is to minimize the discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth joint positions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MPJPE} = \frac{1}{T} \frac{1}{J} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \| \hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}} - \mathbf{y}_{t,j} \|
\end{equation}
\label{eq:mpjpe}
where $\hat{\mathbf{y}_{t,j}}$ and $\mathbf{y}_{t,j}$ are the predicted and ground-truth 3D coordinates of joint $j$ at frame $t$, respectively.

\section{Proposed Method}
We propose a deep learning-based framework for 3D HPE from drone videos named \textbf{Brancher}. The goal of Brancher is to reconstruct the 3D motion of an athlete from monocular aerial 
footage.
First, we introduce the preprocessing step that extracts 2D keypoints from the raw video input. Then, we describe the baseline model used as reference for the ablation study. Finally, we present the main components of Brancher.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
The preprocessing operation is a fundamental step in training with video data. Without it training would be unfeasible due to heavy computational requirements and long computational time.
This operation consists of extracting human's bounding boxes from each frame of the video sequence and extracting the 2D keypoints of the human body from them.
We used YOLOv12 for detecting the human in the input video frames, while for 2D pose estimation, we employ ViTPose \cite{xu2022vitpose}, a high-resolution transformer-based architecture for human keypoint detection.
Given an input frame, this network outputs $J$ heatmaps. Each heatmap represents the spatial probability distribution of a specific joint within the image.
We extract the 2D joint coordinates $(x,y)$ from the heatmaps using an \textit{argmax} operation, identifying the location of the maximum response. We interpret this value as a 
confidence score in the range $[0,1]$, reflecting the reliability of the joint prediction.\\
Then, we normalize the 2D keypoints in the range $x \in [-1, 1]$ and $y \in [-\frac{H}{W}, \frac{H}{W}]$ based on the width $W$ and height $H$ of the frame.\\
We use the normalized joint coordinates and the associated confidence scores as inputs during the training phase of the proposed model.

\subsection{Baseline}
As a baseline for monocular 3D HPE from drone videos, we implemented an architecture based on a \textbf{Temporal Transformer encoder}. The input consists of normalized 2D keypoints for each frame in a temporal 
window of length $T$. 
First, we linear project the 2D joint coordinates into a latent embedding space of dimension $D$ through a fully connected layer. 
We enrich the resulting sequence of embeddings with a standard sinusoidal positional encoding to preserve temporal order, producing a tensor of shape $[T,D]$. 
The encoded sequence is processed by a stack of Transformer encoder layers operating along the temporal dimension. These enable the model to capture motion dynamics and long-range temporal dependencies across frames.
Finally, the latent temporal features are mapped to 3D joint coordinates through a regression head (\textit{PoseHead}), implemented as an MLP with \textit{tanh} activation, which predicts frame-wise 3D joint positions.\\
The baseline effectively models temporal dependencies but presents some limitations. First, the absence of explicit spatial modeling prevents the 
network from capturing structural relationships between joints. Second, the model relies entirely on supervised regression from 2D to 3D poses, without 
leveraging self-supervised constraints or geometric priors. Finally, its design does not explicitly address the domain shift introduced by aerial viewpoints in 
drone-acquired footage.
These limitations motivate the development of the proposed Brancher architecture.

\subsection{Brancher}
\useSvgWithCaption[fig:brancher]{Images/brancher.svg}{1.0}{1.0}{Brancher model architecture}
Brancher is a self-supervised architecture designed for single-person, single-view 3D HPE from drone videos. Starting from normalized 2D keypoints, 
the model first processes the input through a joint spatial-temporal refinement module, learning both structural relationships between joints and motion 
dynamics across frames.
The resulting representation is then fed into a multi-branch refinement stage, with specialized branches that address the key challenges posed by drone footage.

\subsubsection{Spatial and Temporal Module}
A key limitation of purely temporal transformer-based models is that they treat each joint independently across time, without explicitly modeling the structural relationships of the human body. 
However, in drone-based scenarios, strong perspective changes and partial occlusions make it essential to jointly reason about 
\textit{which joints belong together} and \textit{how they move over time}.
To address this issue, we adopt a spatial-temporal attention module inspired by MotionAGFormer \cite{mehraban2024motionagformer}, which follows the MetaFormer design paradigm.
Given an input feature representation \( X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C} \), where \( N \) is the number of tokens and \( C \) the embedding dimension, a MetaFormer block can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    Y = \text{TokenMixer}(\text{Norm}(X)) + X
\end{equation}
where \( \text{Norm}(\cdot) \) denotes a normalization operation and \( \text{TokenMixer}(\cdot) \) represents a generic module that enables information exchange among tokens.
In AGFormer, the token-mixing operation is implemented using two complementary mechanisms: Multi-Head Self-Attention (\textbf{MHSA}) and Graph Convolutional Networks (\textbf{GCNs}). 
The MHSA are responsible for capturing long-range temporal dependencies across the video sequence, enabling the model to reason about motion patterns over multiple frames.
While GCNs explicitly encode the structure of the human skeleton, enforcing anatomical constraints.
This hybrid design allows the network to attend both to \textit{global temporal dynamics} and \textit{local spatial relationships} in a unified framework.
Consequently, the model is more robust to viewpoint changes and noisy 2D detections.

\subsubsection{Multibranch Network}
To robustly model human motion from monocular drone footage, we propose a multibranch architecture inspired by SelfPose \cite{tome2020selfpose}.
Instead of relying on a single regression objective, the model decomposes the problem into multiple correlated tasks, each enforcing complementary geometric and kinematic constraints.

All branches share the same spatial-temporal latent representation and are trained jointly.
This design enables cross-task regularization, improves training stability, and reduces the reliance on fully supervised 3D annotations, which are scarce in real-world aerial scenarios.

\paragraph{Pose Branch}
The Pose Branch is responsible for regressing the 3D joint coordinates from the latent representation produced by the spatial-temporal attention module.
It consists of an MLP followed by a \textit{tanh} activation function, which maps the encoded features to root-relative 3D joint positions.

During training, this branch is supervised by the Mean Per Joint Position Error (\hyperref[eq:mpjpe]{MPJPE}), which penalizes discrepancies with ground-truth 3D poses and provides the strongest supervision signal.
In addition, three self-supervised losses are employed to enforce physical plausibility and temporal consistency of the predicted motion.

The bone length loss, where $O$ is the number of bones in the skeleton, enforces consistency of skeletal proportions by penalizing variations in bone lengths across frames:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{bone} = \frac{1}{TO} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{o=1}^{O} \text{Var}_{t} \bigl( \ell_{o}^{(t)} \bigr)
\end{equation}
The velocity loss encourages smooth motion by penalizing abrupt changes in joint velocities:

{\small
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{vel} &= \frac{1}{(T-2)J} 
    \sum_{t=2}^{T-1} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \\
    &\quad \left\| (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1,j} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j}) 
    - (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j}) \right\|
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
}

Similarly, the acceleration loss penalizes strong variations in joint accelerations, further enforcing temporal smoothness:

{\small
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L}_{acc} &= \frac{1}{(T-3)J}
    \sum_{t=3}^{T-1} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \\
    &\quad \left\| \begin{aligned}
        &(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1,j} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j}) + \\
        &\quad - (\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t,j} - 2\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-1,j} + \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t-2,j})
    \end{aligned} \right\|
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
}

\paragraph{Rotation Branch}
To explicitly enforce geometric consistency, the Rotation Branch predicts a bone-wise rotation matrix with respect to a canonical T-pose. The same relative rotation is independently derived from the 3D pose estimated by the 
Pose Branch, and a consistency loss is applied to align the two, ensuring coherent skeletal orientations.
This branch is implemented as an MLP that outputs a 6D rotation representation for each bone, which is mapped to a valid rotation matrix $ \mathbf{\hat{R}} \in SO(3) $ through Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization.
The 6D representation is chosen for its continuity properties, ensuring stable learning.

Let $ P \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times J \times 3} $ denote the 3D joint predictions.
The function $r(P)$ produces a set of rotation matrices $ \mathbf{R} \in SO(3)^{T \times O} $ by aligning each T-pose bone vector with its corresponding predicted bone vector, where $O$ is the number of bones.
The Rotation Branch is trained using a geodesic loss on rotation matrices together with a consistency loss that applies the predicted rotations to canonical T-pose bones $\mathbf{b}$ and aligns them with the bone directions $\tilde{\mathbf{b}}$ inferred from the Pose Branch.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{geo} = \arccos \left( \frac{\text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}} \mathbf{R}^\top) - 1}{2} \right)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{vec} = \left\| \hat{\mathbf{R}} \mathbf{b}^{\text{T}} - \tilde{\mathbf{b}} \right\|_1
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Uncertainty Branch}
The Uncertainty Branch models joint-wise prediction uncertainty. This module estimates a probability distribution for each joint, increasing the network's resilience to unreliable predictions and improving robustness to noisy aerial 
data. For each joint $j$, the branch yields: 
a mean heatmap $\mu_j$ representing the estimated spatial probability distribution of the joint location, and a log-variance term $s_j = \log \sigma^2_j$, modeling heteroscedastic uncertainty.
Predicted heatmaps are converted into differentiable joint coordinates through a spatial \textit{soft-argmax} operation.
The branch is optimized using an aleatoric regression loss applied to the predicted coordinates that balances prediction accuracy with uncertainty estimation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{hm} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J} c_j \left( e^{-s_j} \| \mathbf{x}_j - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j \|^2 + s_j \right)
\end{equation}
where $x_j$ denotes the soft-argmax predicted coordinates, $\tilde{x_j}$ the target 2D keypoints, and $c_j$ is a confidence weight derived from the preprocessing stage. The precision term $e^{-s_j}$
adaptively scales the squared error, while $s_j$ prevents trivial solutions with unbounded uncertainty.

\subsection{Loss Weighting}
\paragraph{Supervised Pretraining.}
During supervised pretraining, each loss term is first normalized by a fixed scale factor $a_i$. The normalized losses are then combined through adaptive importance weights obtained from a learnable \textit{softmax} parametrization 
$SM(\mathbf{z})$, together with a temporal gating mechanism $g_i(t)$ and a dynamic floor term $f(t)$:
\begin{equation} 
    \begin{aligned} 
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{pretrain}} &= \sum_{i} \Big[ \mathrm{SM}(\alpha_i) (1 - f(t)) +
        \\ &\qquad + \delta_{i,0} f(t) \Big] \, g_i(t) \, \frac{\mathcal{L}_i}{a_i} 
    \end{aligned} 
\end{equation}
Here, $\alpha_i$ are learnable parameters. The function $f(t)$ enforces MPJPE dominance during the early epochs, $\delta_{i,0}$ is a Kronecker delta equal to 1 if $i=\text{MPJPE}$ and 0 otherwise, and $g_i(t)$ progressively activates the secondary objectives.

\paragraph{Pure Self-Supervised Training.}
In the purely self-supervised setting, we adopt the homoscedastic uncertainty formulation, introducing learnable parameters $s_i = \log \sigma_i^2$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{self}} =
\sum_{i}
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
e^{-s_i}\mathcal{L}_i + s_i
\right)
\end{equation}
where $e^{-s_i}$ is the adaptive precision term, and $s_i$ acts as a regularizer preventing trivial solutions.

\section{Experiments}
The baseline model is trained from scratch on AthletePose3D \cite{yeung2025athletepose3d} and serves as a reference for evaluating the impact of architectural and training modifications.
Brancher follows a two-stage training strategy. First, it is initialized with the weights of AGFormer-Small \cite{mehraban2024motionagformer} and fine-tuned on AthletePose3D to adapt to fast and complex sports motions. 
In the second stage, Brancher is trained entirely in a self-supervised manner on UAV datasets, including AirPose dataset \cite{saini2022airpose} and our custom drone dataset. In this phase, the supervised \hyperref[eq:mpjpe]{MPJPE} 
loss is disabled, and the model is optimized without 3D ground-truth annotations, enforcing adaptation to aerial viewpoints and real-world drone artifacts.
\\\\
Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes the quantitative comparison and provides an ablation analysis of the proposed architecture.
The original AGFormer exhibits high error, confirming that sota 3D pose estimators trained on generic datasets do not directly generalize to fast and highly dynamic sports motions. Fine-tuning on 
AthletePose3D (AGFormer-FT) drastically reduces MPJPE, achieving the lowest error overall. This result highlights the importance of large-scale 3D pretraining followed by domain-specific supervised 
adaptation.

The ablation study clarifies the contribution of each architectural component. Brancher with only the PoseBranch significantly improves performance over the Baseline, and incorporating the RotationBranch 
further reduces MPJPE. When the UncertaintyBranch is introduced, MPJPE increases, as the optimization is no longer driven exclusively by direct 3D supervision but instead balances multiple 
objectives.

Although this increase in MPJPE may appear detrimental from a purely numerical perspective, qualitative inspection reveals a different behavior. The Baseline and AGFormer-FT, despite achieving competitive 
benchmark scores, fail to reproduce coherent 3D motion under UAV viewpoints and exhibit unstable or unrealistic reconstructions. In contrast, models equipped with the UncertaintyBranch generate smoother and 
temporally more consistent motion, better reflecting realistic human dynamics captured from aerial cameras.

Finally, Brancher-Self exhibits higher error than Brancher. This is expected, as the additional self-supervised training on UAV data shifts the optimization away from strict minimization of the AthletePose3D benchmark. As a result, 
in-domain accuracy slightly decreases, while cross-domain generalization to aerial videos improves.

\section{Conclusions}
We presented a deep learning framework for 3D HPE from sports aerial videos, a scenario still largely underexplored. The proposed approach integrates spatio-temporal joint modeling with 
uncertainty-aware learning, enabling reconstruction of complex 3D motions from challenging drone footage.
By jointly optimizing 2D consistency, rotational alignment, and prediction uncertainty, the model mitigates the effects of occlusions, viewpoint variations, and noisy pseudo-labels, improving motion 
stability even under limited subject visibility.
As future work, the creation of a UAV-based dataset with 3D annotations would enable more rigorous evaluation and further advance research in aerial 3D HPE.
